How I used data mining to find students who may fail

A beginner project in Python, from raw attendance and marks to a list of students who need help early.

By Emad Ali, 28 October 2026. About a 6 minute read.

In short

  • Goal: spot students at risk of failing before the exam.
  • Data: 500 students, generated with Python.
  • Result: about 80 to 82% accuracy, and attendance mattered most.
  • Tools: Python, pandas, scikit-learn and matplotlib.

Why I built this

In most colleges a student’s problem becomes visible only after the result is declared. By then it is too late to help. But attendance and marks are recorded all semester, so I asked a simple question: could those records warn us earlier?

That is what data mining is for. It finds useful patterns in data that already exists. My plan was to follow the usual steps: collect, clean, explore, mine and then use the results.

The data

Real student records are private, so I generated 500 students with Python. Each one has attendance, internal marks, assignment completion, study hours per day and previous GPA, plus a Pass or Fail label. About 28% of the students are labelled Fail. I also removed ten study-hours values on purpose, to practise handling missing data.

Important: because the data is synthetic, my numbers show how the method works. They do not describe how real students behave.

Step 1: Clean and explore

Missing study hours were filled with the median value, and duplicate rows were removed. Then I plotted attendance against internal marks. Failing students cluster in the low-attendance, low-marks corner, which is what I expected.

Step 2: Predict who will fail

I split the data into 75% for training and 25% for testing, then trained three classifiers: a Decision Tree, a Random Forest and Naive Bayes.

X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(

model = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, random_state=42)

print(accuracy_score(y_te, model.predict(X_te)))

The scores are close, so the choice of model mattered less than I expected. The catch is in the details: for the Random Forest, 17 failing students were found but 18 were missed. At the default setting the models catch only about half of the students who fail.

Step 3: What matters most?

The Random Forest can tell you how much each column helped its decisions. Attendance came first, then internal marks.

Step 4: Group similar students

K-Means clustering sorts students into groups without using the Pass or Fail label. With three clusters, the groups turned out very different:

This was my favourite result. It shows that at-risk students need different kinds of help: one group needs attendance counselling, another needs academic support.

Step 5: Find rules

Association rules look for conditions that appear together. I used three measures: support (how often the rule applies), confidence (how often the outcome follows) and lift (how much more likely it is than normal).

RuleConfidenceLift
Low attendance and low study hours, then Fail0.913.26
Low attendance and low internal marks, then Fail0.853.02
Low attendance, then Fail0.642.29

Students with both low attendance and low study hours failed 91% of the time, about three times the overall fail rate. Rules like this are easy to explain to a teacher, which is a big advantage.

Step 6: Make it useful

The last step turns results into action. The program ranks every student by failure probability and saves the top 25 to a CSV file, so a mentor knows who to talk to first.

What I learned

  • Accuracy is not enough. A model can score 80% and still miss half of the students you most want to find. Recall matters.
  • Clusters tell a story. Grouping students showed me different types of risk that one prediction number hides.
  • Simple is good. A short rule or a decision tree is easier to trust than a black box.
  • Be honest about the data. Synthetic data is fine for learning, but it cannot prove anything about real students.

Limits and next steps

The data is synthetic and small, and it cannot capture reasons such as illness or family problems. A prediction is only a probability, so it should start a conversation with a student and never label them. Next, I want to try this on a real, anonymised dataset and tune the model to catch more of the students who fail.

Try it yourself
